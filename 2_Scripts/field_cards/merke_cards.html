Bait: one size does not fit this community
  • Shrews are insectivores. The lesser white-toothed shrew eats over 100% of its body weight daily, so a seed bait does not feed it and it starves quickly in a trap. An animal-protein component and a short check interval both matter.
  • Voles and the dwarf fat-tailed jerboa are folivores, eating green material rather than seed.
  • The eastern mole vole eats underground tubers and rarely comes to the surface at all.
  • Brown rats are neophobic and may avoid a new trap for a night or two, which argues for pre-baiting.

Habitat

Arid habitats such as dry grassland, steppe, desert, semi-desert, and alpine meadows at elevations up to 3600 m. In Mongolia, Gray Dwarf Hamsters occupy hills and mountains, oases, and valleys with shrubs and herbaceous plants in deserts and semi-deserts. They also occur in agricultural land and gardens.

Activity patterns

Gray Dwarf Hamsters are nocturnal and crepuscular and sometimes active during the day even when temperatures are low. They usually do not hibernate.

Burrow

It digs burrow systems with nesting chambers, food storage areas, and 2-5 entrances. Burrows can be 1-5 m deep and 2 m long. It also uses natural holes and fissures in soil.

Food and Feeding

The Gray Dwarf Hamster eats roots, shoots, seeds, small fruits, green vegetation, snails, insects, and rarely small reptiles, amphibians, and other rodents. The Gray Dwarf Hamster stores up to 400-500 g of food in storage chambers and gathers plants to store in "hay piles" for winter use.

Habitat

Arid areas with shrubby slopes, dry forests, mountain steppes, and foothills and southern slopes of mountains to elevations of c.1900 m. The Long-tailed Dwarf Hamster is particularly abundant in piedmont semi-desert, a type of desert grassland with mixed shrubs and succulent plants.

Activity patterns

The Long-tailed Dwarf Hamster is nocturnal and does not hibernate.

Burrow

The Long-tailed Dwarf Hamster occupies shallow, simple burrows that extend horizontally beneath the ground's surface, often constructed under rocks. Abandoned burrows of other small mammals are sometimes used, and side passages are created leading to grass-lined nest chambers.

Food and Feeding

The Long-tailed Dwarf Hamster eats plant material, mainly seeds, and insects. In late summer, it gathers and stores plants in hay piles for winter use.

Habitat

Well-drained open landscapes, typically grasslands and pastures within forest zone and alpine meadows above tree line up to elevations of c.3000 m. This Common Vole can be abundant in grain and alfalfa fields and is regarded as an agricultural pest. In short-grass habitat, it is exposed to avian predation, and therefore it seeks protection by digging underground galleries.

Activity patterns

Common Voles are active year-round and do not hibernate. Surface activity is crepuscular and diurnal, depending on season and density. In winter, they are more active during day.

Burrow

Burrows are usually short (100-150 cm long and 35 cm deep) and terminate in nest chamber and caches. Nests (8-15 cm in diameter) are woven of dry grass and are 5-15 cm below the ground's surface; they usually have 2-3 exits.

Food and Feeding

Diet consists of leaves, stems, and roots of various herbs and grasses. More than 80 plants have been identified in its diet. Depending on season, they also eat fungi, lichens, berries, nuts, seeds, bulbs, tubers, and bark. Daily consumption is c.3-6 g of dry matter.

Habitat

Semi-deserts and deserts on plains and in hilly regions, steppes, alpine pastures, and rocky slopes in mountains and on high plateaus. In the Tian Shan and on the Pamir Mountains, Eastern Mole Voles have been recorded up to elevations of c.4000 m. Humid places in valleys, along streams, and around lakes are preferred; moving sands are avoided.

Activity patterns

Eastern Mole Voles are active day and night and year-round. Annual peaks of digging are in March-April and September-October, and daily peaks are at 06:00-10:00 h and 14:00-17:00 h. With increase in elevation, activity is progressively more diurnal, giving individuals advantage of higher soil temperatures. Eastern Mole Voles are fossorial and spend little time aboveground.

Burrow

They live in complex burrow systems, with one or several main tunnels and numerous short side branches. Tunnels are 5-6 cm in diameter and usually at depths of 5-40 cm. About 30-40% of tunnels are plugged with soil. Mounds contain 310-4750 g (mean 1698 g) of excavated soil. Although entrances to tunnels are sealed by mounds of soil, individuals occasionally open them, presumably for ventilation.

Food and Feeding

Eastern Mole Voles feed on underground plant parts, especially starchy bulbs and tubers. Frequencies of various diet items in stomach samples were: underground plant parts 94-9%, green plant parts 39-2%, seeds 0-5%, bark 0-3%, and animals 0-5%.

Habitat

Tundra, forest-tundra, coniferous forest, forest steppe, steppe, semi-desert and desert zones, and mountain meadows up to elevations of 4200 m. In tundra, semidesert, and desert, Narrow-headed Voles prefer river valleys and lake depressions. In forests, they occur in open dry meadows.

Activity patterns

Activity patterns of Narrow-headed Voles vary seasonally. In spring, they are active round-the-clock, appearing aboveground in short bouts 100-200 times/day. In summer, they are more active at twilight, with four-minute activity bouts, and diurnal bouts of aboveground activity last 2-4 minutes every two hours. With decreasing temperatures in autumn, night activity decreases and daily activity increases to 15 minutes/bout.

Burrow

Family burrows have several large nest chambers, 12-16 cm in diameter and c.12 cm deep in summer and 21 cm deep in winter. A burrow also contains several storage chambers and system of tunnels at depths of 7-9 cm. Burrow systems of average family groups have an average of 17 entrances. Larger burrows with 100-120 entrances were also recorded.

Food and Feeding

Diet varies seasonally, but in every season, green parts of grasses, onions, and forbs are eaten. In early autumn, seeds, berries, fungi, and roots make up 7-10% of diet.

Habitat

Rocky areas, talus slopes, and accumulations of boulders at elevations of 500-4300 m. Silver Mountain Voles avoid dense vegetation, and southern exposures are preferred.

Activity patterns

Silver Mountain Voles are diurnal, with peaks in activity at 05:00-07:00 h and 18:00-20:00 h. They are active throughout the year. They do not dig burrows but seek shelter in fissures and cracks. Rocky shelters can be protected against adverse environmental conditions by walls constructed of stones, excrement, and urine. A shelter found in Kazakhstan was 1 m long and 10-18 cm high.

Food and Feeding

Silver Mountain Voles eat primarily roots and seeds in winter and green plants for the rest of the year. When vegetation starts growing, plants are collected, dried, and cached for winter. Seeds are transported in cheek pouches.

Habitat

Semi-desert and desert habitat in patches of sparse saltwort vegetation; in western part of the distribution, only on clay soils; in northern and eastern parts of the distribution, on clay and gravel soils.

Activity patterns

The Dwarf Fat-tailed Jerboa is crepuscular and nocturnal. Aboveground activity usually starts 40-60 minutes after sunset in spring and autumn and 20-30 minutes after sunset in summer. Activity ends immediately before sunrise. Activity has 2-3 peaks, in the first one-half, middle, and end of the night.

Burrow

Summer burrows usually consist of the initial tunnel filled with soil; nest chambers are 6-7 cm in diameter for males and non-breeding females, 10-12 cm for breeding females, and 37-51 cm deep. Night shelter burrows are simple, with one tunnel 25-90 cm long.

Food and Feeding

The Dwarf Fat-tailed Jerboa is mainly folivorous. Green plant parts dominate its diet in all parts of its distribution and in almost all seasons, comprising 60-90% by volume. Seeds can be 8-25% of diets. Insects are always minor parts of the diet.

Habitat

Forest steppe, steppe, semi-desert, and desert. In forest steppe and steppe, the Great Jerboa selects open areas with sparse grass cover such as pastures, along sides of dirt roads, crop fields, shores of salt lakes, and high terraces of river valleys.

Activity patterns

The Great Jerboa is nocturnal. Aboveground activity usually starts 40-45 minutes after sunset and ends 0-5-1-5 hours before sunrise, with peak activity near end of the first one-half of night. Hibernation lasts from as early as the end of September to April.

Burrow

Summer burrows usually have two main tunnels branching off the initial tunnel; nest chambers are 9-13 cm in diameter and 40-100 cm deep (usually 50-70 cm). Night shelter burrows are simple, with one tunnel 80-200 cm long.

Food and Feeding

The Great Jerboa is omnivorous. It eats seeds, insects, and green and underground parts of plants in equal amounts.

Habitat

Sand massifs at different stages of sand stabilization in desert, semi-desert, and steppe zones but always with patches of pure sand without grass or forb vegetation; in the south-eastern distribution, sands, sandy-gravel, and gravel plains in desert zones.

Activity patterns

The Northern Three-toed Jerboa is crepuscular and nocturnal. Aboveground activity starts at sunset in summer and 20-90 minutes after sunset in spring and autumn. In autumn, individuals are active only during the first one-half of the night, and length of activity can decrease to one hour. Hibernation in Russian Dagestan and Kazakhstan lasts 4-5 months from end of October to mid-March.

Burrow

Burrows are simple and have one main entrance, one emergency entrance (dug up from the inside to 5-7 cm below the ground's surface), and one nest chamber 5-7 cm in diameter at depths of 35-110 cm; total lengths of tunnels are 50-400 cm. Main entrance in spring and summer usually is closed with sand plug.

Food and Feeding

Diets contain seeds and flowers (36-73%, average 51%), green plant material (16-54%, average 35%), roots and bulbs (0-40%, average 10%), and insects (1-11%, average 4%).

Habitat

Desert and semi-desert zones, avoiding only large open spaces completely lacking shrubs and areas with very dense vegetation. The Small Five-toed Jerboa does not live in sand but is able to cross sandy areas and occupy small patches of hard soil in large sand massifs.

Activity patterns

The Small Five-toed Jerboa is crepuscular and nocturnal. Most individuals become active at dusk, 15-25 minutes after sunset. In spring and summer, individuals are active all night, but in autumn, their activity is usually limited to the first one-half of the night. Hibernation in Kazakhstan and Central Asia lasts 2-5—4-5 months from mid-October to early December to mid-February/March, with interruptions during thaws.

Burrow

Summer burrows are simple and usually have one main tunnel starting at the ground's surface, typically with a plugged entrance and ending in a nest chamber 10-13 cm in diameter and 10-115 cm deep (usually 40-70 cm); lengths of tunnels are 45-300 cm. In most cases, burrows have 1-2 additional tunnels ending with emergency exits and several (up to seven) additional chambers. Small Five-toed Jerboas usually do not build special night shelter burrows but use burrows of other rodents as night shelters.

Food and Feeding

In northern parts of the distribution, they feed mainly (c.80% by volume) on seeds, but in southern parts, seeds and green plant parts are eaten in equal proportions. Insects and underground plant parts are consistent but minor parts of the diet.

Habitat

Dry riverbeds, dunes, oases, valleys with shrubs, semiarid country, and steppes over most of its distribution. It favors mesic sites such as suburban parks and gardens, olive groves, and agricultural lands and is commonly associated with humans. It selects soft ground such as sandy dunes where it can dig relatively deep burrows that slope at a slight angle.

Activity patterns

The Common Long-eared Hedgehog is nocturnal and begins foraging early in the evening. Day-resting sites usually are in burrows that are under small shrubs or bushes. Burrows are 20-150 cm long and have a single opening. It can enter torpor in any season during droughts, shortages of food, and cold or hot weather.

Food and Feeding

Activity patterns

Tian Shan Birch Mice are mainly crepuscular and nocturnal, although they are sometimes active during the day, particularly young individuals. Pitfall traps after a cold night, normal in the high mountains, regularly contained individuals lying curled on their side with closed eyes, seemingly in a state of torpor. They usually quickly became active when warmed in the hand. Adults probably enter hibernation in August because only juveniles have been captured in September.

Food and Feeding

Tian Shan Birch Mice mostly eat insects and other animals such as snails, followed by seeds; fewer berries and herbs were found in stomachs. Proportion of insects and other animal food decreased from June to September, and quantities of seeds and berries increased.

Habitat

Desert, steppe, and forest zones, preferring habitats with bushes or high grass stands near watersides and ruderal vegetation at sites of abandoned human houses and tilled fields. The Lesser White-toothed Shrew is found in various human dwellings such as nomadic tents in the Gobi Desert and apartments in Moscow high-rise apartment buildings, including rural houses from European Russia to Tian Shan.

Activity patterns

The Lesser White-toothed Shrew is active mostly at night. In Turkmenistan, 10-14 activity periods were observed during dark hours and averaged 24 minutes each; maximal duration of an activity period was 155 minutes.

Food and Feeding

In Trans-Ili Alatau, small beetles are most common in diets of Lesser White-toothed Shrews. Lesser White-toothed Shrews can dig insects out of the soil to up to 2 cm and hunt small geometrid moths by jumping and collecting them from grass blades. Daily food intake of Lesser White-toothed Shrews reaches 133% of body weight, but daily food intake of three times body weight was observed in Turkmenistan.

Habitat

Large variety of alpine, subalpine, and arctic habitats, shrublands, and meadows, often dominated by small trees and shrubs such as willow and birch, generally preferring open bush habitats. It prefers to live in floodplain regions throughout taiga, avoiding forests, and in open areas between forests.

Activity patterns

Tundra Shrews are active day and night and are primarily terrestrial.

Food and Feeding

Because Tundra Shrews live in various habitats, food items differ greatly among different geographical regions. For example, earthworms account for substantial proportions of diets in tundra, spiders and insects (commonly beetles) in taiga, and grasshoppers in steppe regions. Plant items are often included in diets, especially in winter; berries are eaten in southern regions of the distribution in Kazakhstan.
